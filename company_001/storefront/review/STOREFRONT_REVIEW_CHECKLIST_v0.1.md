# Storefront Review Checklist v0.1

**Review object:** Storefront Zero  
**Question:** Does this company deserve a customer's trust before they have bought anything?  
**Status vocabulary:** Pass / Conditional / Fail / Not tested  
**Rule:** Appearance alone cannot earn a pass.

## Review discipline

Reviewers should record what is visible, what they infer, and what remains unknown separately. A polished prototype is not evidence of product quality, demand, supplier performance, price, fulfillment, or customer trust.

For every finding, capture:

- Page and section.
- Exact element or wording.
- Observed response.
- Why it matters.
- Severity: blocking / material / minor.
- Proposed action: keep / revise / remove / test.
- Evidence needed before the action becomes permanent.

## 1. Five-Second Test

A first-time visitor should immediately understand:

- What the company sells.
- Why it is different.
- Whether the visitor is in the right place.

Review prompts:

1. After five seconds, write the visitor's likely one-sentence description of the company.
2. Name the product category without using navigation or scrolling.
3. Name the differentiation without repeating a slogan.
4. Identify who should leave immediately.
5. Mark any competing headline, label, or prototype disclaimer that slows comprehension.

**Pass condition:** all three questions are answered accurately from the first viewport without explanation from the founder.

## 2. Trust Test

Before scrolling, ask: would I trust this company with my money because it feels honest?

Look for:

- Clear prototype and candidate-product status.
- No false price, availability, review, performance, or urgency claim.
- A visible reason to believe the company will help reject a bad purchase.
- Language that distinguishes evidence from intention.
- A visual presentation that feels careful without pretending to be established.

Failure signals:

- Trust language unsupported by behavior.
- Premium styling used as a substitute for product evidence.
- Certainty that exceeds the current stage.
- Important limitations hidden below persuasive copy.

## 3. Delete Test

Evaluate the experience with:

1. all three candidates;
2. one candidate hidden;
3. two candidates hidden.

For each state, ask:

- Does the promise still make sense?
- Does navigation still make sense?
- Does the About page still describe the company accurately?
- Does the remaining collection still feel intentional?
- Is the company stronger than the catalog?

**Pass condition:** the brand promise and recommendation behavior remain coherent with one product. This does not prove customer demand.

## 4. Recommendation Test

Every product page must answer:

- Why this product?
- Why not a cheaper alternative?
- Why not a premium alternative?
- Who should not buy it?

It should also expose:

- What problem the product cannot solve.
- What remains unknown.
- What must be verified before recommendation.
- Whether fit can be known before purchase.

**Pass condition:** a skeptical customer can make a better go/no-go decision without relying on a rating, testimonial, or sales claim.

## 5. Exit Test

If the visitor leaves without buying, did they leave smarter?

Look for transferable knowledge:

- Diagnose before adding storage.
- Measure the real constraint.
- Recognize when a cheaper solution is enough.
- Recognize when a permanent solution is justified.
- Identify hazards or conditions a storage product cannot repair.

**Pass condition:** the visitor gains at least one useful decision rule that remains valuable without a purchase.

## Brand decision gate

For Storefront Zero, **Little Built** remains an acceptable internal prototype identity. Before any public exposure, explicitly choose one of:

1. a new Little Built division;
2. a standalone consumer brand;
3. an internal prototype identity that will not launch.

Do not interpret use in this prototype as approval for public launch. Before selection, review customer expectation, existing service-business reputation, legal availability, channel fit, future category expansion, and the cost of later separation.

The promise **“Small changes. Better everyday living.”** remains a working constraint. Every launch candidate must reinforce it; products that require disruptive installation, create avoidable commitment, or cannot support better everyday decisions need explicit contrary evidence to enter Company 001.
